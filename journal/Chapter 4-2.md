# Chapter 4 - Knowing What to Do (Part 2)

The second part of chapter 4 continues talking about constraints and affordances in design. However, talking about the new stuff, it started talking about forcing functions. This being preventing incorrect usage of an object. We go into specific constraints including interlocks, lock-ins and lockouts. It took me a minute to wrap my head around these three lowkey, I think it was because they were all rather similar in that they were all some form of a constraint. The examples that Norman used were very helpful like dead-man's switch or the save your work confirmation on page 142-143. Interlocks are locks that prevent you from continuing until you fulfill a condition, like the dead-man's switch which forces you to apply pressure on the object in order for the action to be performed and maintained. Lock-ins I understood to be literally locking the user into continuing to use the object, like the save screen of an application double-checking whether you really want to stop using it or not. Lastly, lock-outs are used to lock you out of places or actions you're not supposed to be or do, like a fence on private property. 

The next part Norman talks about is conventions or in my definition, human perception. People are trained and have learned to expect how certain objects should be used and how to interact with them. When your design goes against traditional expectations for how your design should behave, it becomes frustrating and disorienting to adjust to. Prime example of this being the destination-control elevator on page 148 which I still have a hard time picturing how to use it despite reading its description. It's a simple fact that standardized design makes your product easier to learn and use. On a random note, ever wonder why the hot water for a sink is the left lever and not the right? I think it's crazy that basically the entire human population of the US have just agreed that the left lever is hot water
